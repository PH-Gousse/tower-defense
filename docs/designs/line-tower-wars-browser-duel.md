# Design: Line Tower Wars — 1v1 / 1 vs AI

Generated by /office-hours on 2026-09-07, revised in /plan-ceo-review same day
Branch: main
Repo: PH-Gousse/tower-defense (working dir: `Game`)
Status: APPROVED
Mode: Builder

## Problem Statement

A tower-wars game: tower defence where the enemies aren't scripted waves but creeps the
other player sends at you. You are attacker and defender at the same time, against one
opponent — human or AI bot.

Warcraft 3's Line Tower Wars did this and nothing browser-native has replaced it. The
browser TD field is solo wave defence on a fixed track. Both halves of LTW — free-form
mazing and the send/income duel — belong in the browser, where the second player is one URL
away and, failing that, is a bot.

## What Makes This Cool

**The duel, not the towers.** Income arrives in a lump every 15 seconds, and each lump is a
fork: towers to survive what's coming, or creeps to pressure them and compound your income.
Turtling loses the money war; over-sending leaves you exposed. Income and threat are the
same currency, and the decision cadence is roughly four times a minute, not continuous.

**Leaks are a drain, not a penalty.** A creep that beats your maze loops and beats it again,
taking a life every lap, until your towers kill it. One bad leak is a crisis you have time to
answer, not a point you lost. That single rule is what makes the game tense rather than
arithmetic.

**Counter-picking is direct.** You see their maze and send what exploits it. They see what's
coming and adapt. No hidden information, all reading.

**The link is the product.** "Click this" is the difference between a game people play and a
repo people star. The room URL lives permanently in the HUD. When nobody clicks it, the bot
is there.

## Constraints

- Solo developer working with an AI coding agent. Implementation compresses ~10-20x vs a
  human team; design decisions do not compress at all.
- Side project, built for fun. No deadline, no revenue target, no user to answer to.
- Empty repo, no stack chosen, no prior code to reuse.

## Premises

1. **The duel is the point.** Without an opponent choosing what to throw at you, this is one
   more wave-defense game. The AI bot exists so that opponent always exists.
2. **Distribution is a launch-blocking feature, not polish.** A URL that drops a friend into
   a lane in under 10 seconds is scope. A browser game with no deployed URL does not exist —
   which is why deploy is step 1, not step 10.
3. **Determinism is the one expensive-to-reverse decision.** Pure `step()`, seeded PRNG,
   arithmetic restricted to `+ - * / sqrt` (bit-exact everywhere under IEEE 754), hard
   sim/render boundary, command log as the source of truth. Everything else falls out of it:
   replays are a seed plus a command log re-run to any tick; spectators consume the same
   stream; the AI is just another command source; and the whole game is testable headlessly.
4. **Free-form mazing is the identity.** Non-negotiable. It is what the player is actually
   doing with their hands.
5. **Data-driven from the start.** Towers, creeps, tiers, and economy constants live in data
   files. Balancing a duel means changing numbers hundreds of times.

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Why |
|---|----------|-----|
| 1 | Full PvP send-units duel, plus an AI opponent | The send layer is the product. The bot means it is playable at 11pm alone |
| 2 | Browser + shareable room link | Only distribution that survives contact with a real friend; rejected desktop (download friction), Discord activity (platform dependency), local hotseat (undemonstrable remotely) |
| 3 | Free-form mazing, block refused at placement | It is the genre. Refusal at build time teaches the rule instantly; rejected WC3's patrolling anti-block NPC and creeps-attack-walls |
| 4 | 1v1 now, team-shaped data model | Lives/gold belong to a `Team` with one member. Priced YAGNI: one indirection today, a rewrite later |
| 5 | **Floats, with arithmetic restricted to `+ - * / sqrt`** | Those five are bit-exact across engines under IEEE 754. Transcendentals (`sin`, `cos`, `pow`, `exp`) differ by libm and are lint-banned. *(Final position after Q16.16 fixed-point → floats → integers → floats. The integer variant was stronger in principle — determinism by construction rather than by lint rule — but the builder's call is floats, and the cross-engine golden fixture is what keeps the lint rule honest.)* |
| 6 | Flow field, not per-creep A\* | Creep population is bounded only by gold. Per-creep A\* is O(creeps × search) per placement; a flow field is one integer Dijkstra from the exit per field, O(tiles). It also yields the no-block check, the path-preview delta, the maze score and unambiguous targeting for free |
| 7 | Cloudflare Workers + Durable Objects for the relay; no `ws`, no Colyseus in v1 | `ws` is a Node library and does not run on Workers — the relay uses `WebSocketPair` and the Hibernation API. | 8 | **pnpm monorepo: `sim` / `client` / `server` / `harness`** | The Workers relay and the Node harness both import the same sim the browser runs — precisely the trigger the earlier single-app decision named ("split out when something else needs to import the sim"). It is also what lets the server become authoritative later with no rewrite |
| 9 | Three towers × three levels, three creep types, no damage/armour types in v1 | Clean rock-paper-scissors. Elements, research, abilities and armour types layer on later |
| 10 | **Bot emits `Input` records, never mutates state** | A saved bot match then replays on any future version, and the bot becomes unit-testable in isolation |

## Simulation Contract

This section is the spec. Everything above is rationale.

### World

- **Two lanes**, one per player, mirrored. Each is a **40 × 24 tile** grid.
- **Spawn:** tiles at `x=0, y=11..12`. **Exit:** tiles at `x=39, y=11..12`.
- You build only in your own lane. You can see both — your own at full size, the opponent's
  as a live scaled view (see Interface).
- **Tower footprint:** 1 × 1. A tile holds at most one tower.
- **Creeps** do not collide and do not block tiles. Only towers are obstacles.
- **Creep ownership vs location:** a creep has an `owner` (the sender, for scoring) and a
  `lane` (the defender's field, where it physically exists). A creep never appears in its
  owner's lane.
- **A tower may be placed on a tile a creep occupies.** Creeps are not obstacles, so nothing
  stops it. The creep reroutes; if it has no route at all, see Pathing.
- **A send enqueues creeps in the opponent's lane; the lane releases one every 4 ticks.**
  A send never spawns its creeps simultaneously. Six identical Swarms entering on the same tick
  at the same tile would never separate — creeps do not collide, movement is deterministic, and
  they all read the same `dir` from the same tile — so they would travel as a single point
  forever: one dot on screen, and one splash hit killing all six. That collapses the Splash
  tower's entire reason to exist. A per-lane spawn queue fixes it deterministically, without
  the randomness the sim does not have. The 4-tick interval is a tuning constant.
- **Released creeps alternate between the two spawn tiles** (`y=11`, then `y=12`, by release
  order). This is a determinism-critical ordering point.

### Determinism

`packages/sim` is pure TypeScript with **zero three.js and zero DOM dependency**. It takes a
list of commands and advances one tick. Nothing in it reads the clock, the network, or the
document.

**Arithmetic is restricted to `+ - * / sqrt`.** IEEE 754 pins those five to bit-exact results
on every engine. Transcendentals do not — `sin`, `cos`, `tan`, `pow`, `exp`, `log` and
`hypot` are implemented by each platform's libm and differ in the last bits.

Enforced in `packages/sim` by ESLint:

- `no-restricted-properties` on `Math`, allowing only `abs, min, max, floor, ceil, round,
  trunc, sign, sqrt`
- `no-restricted-globals` on `Math` as well, to close the aliasing path (`const m = Math;
  m.sin(x)`) and destructuring, which property-matching alone cannot see
- the `**` operator banned — it is `Math.pow` spelled differently
- `Date.now` and `Math.random` banned outright
- `Object.keys` / `values` / `entries`, `for...in`, `JSON.stringify`, and
  `Array.prototype.sort` without a comparator banned — unordered iteration is a determinism
  risk independent of arithmetic

**The lint rule is not airtight and is not the guarantee.** The cross-engine golden fixture is
(see Tests). The rules make violations unlikely; the fixture makes them visible.

**Ordered iteration points that must be pinned:** flow-field neighbours (N, E, S, W),
spatial-hash bucket iteration (bucket index ascending, then creep id), tower firing order
(tile index ascending), creep update order (creep id ascending), and **spawn-queue release
order** (queue order, alternating spawn tiles).

**`step()` returns a new state; it does not mutate in place.** The renderer needs the previous
tick to interpolate against, so the driver keeps the last two states. At ~500 creeps that is
double-buffering two typed-array-backed structures, not two object graphs — allocate the pair
once and swap, rather than allocating per tick.

**RNG.** Nothing in v1's rules consumes randomness — no crits, no spawn jitter, no random
targeting. `rng.ts` (mulberry32, seeded) exists solely for the bot to break ties between
equally-rated moves, and the match seed exists so those tie-breaks replay. If the bot ends up
not needing it, delete both.

### Pathing — flow field

One **integer Dijkstra from the exit tiles** across each field, recomputed when that field's
tower set changes. Each tile stores `dist` (tiles to exit) and `dir` (neighbour to step
toward). Creeps read `dir` at their current tile; they do not own paths.

- **Neighbours are 4-connected, iterated in fixed order N, E, S, W.** With integer distances
  and a fixed order the field is uniquely determined — no tiebreak comparator needed.
- **Cost:** O(tiles) = 960 per rebuild, on a discrete placement event. Independent of creep
  count, which is the whole reason for this choice.
- **No-block check is free.** After a candidate rebuild, if either spawn tile has infinite
  `dist`, the placement is refused. No separate flood fill.
- **Path preview is free.** Hovering reports `newDist - oldDist` at the spawn tile: the
  `+54 tiles` teaching signal.
- **Maze score is free.** The spawn tile's `dist` is the maze length.

**A creep with no path teleports to its lane's spawn.** No per-creep placement check, no
refusal, no snap ordering, no assert. If a field rebuild leaves a creep on a tile with infinite
`dist`, it reappears at the spawn and continues, keeping its damage and its lap count.

**This is the entire pocket-trap fix.** Walling a creep in costs the trapper towers and
achieves nothing, so trapping is never worth doing — which was always the actual requirement.
Two earlier designs were tried and both were worse:

- *Pricing the trap at three lives* left it profitable for any creep surviving more than three
  laps — precisely the Tank, whose design purpose is outlasting your maze. Any fixed penalty is
  beaten by a creep that outlives it.
- *Refusing the placement* made build legality depend on where enemy creeps were standing. That
  makes the rules flicker in the one mechanic the game is about, turns cheap Swarm sends into a
  build-denial weapon that scales with how badly the defender is already losing, and breaks
  local prediction — legality is evaluated at click time but applied up to a second later, by
  which point creeps have moved.

Render the jump-back visibly, or it reads as a bug rather than a rule.

### Targeting — spatial hash

Creeps are bucketed by tile, rebuilt each tick. A tower gathers candidates from the buckets
its range covers.

**Target selection: the in-range creep with the lowest `dist`, tiebroken by creep id
ascending.** The flow field already computed `dist`, so this is one integer lookup.

Known emergent consequence, unresolved: towers permanently focus whichever creep is nearest
the exit, typically a long-lived looping tank. Fresh swarms walk behind it unharassed. This
may be a feature (the tank soaks aggro, so swarms are the correct follow-up) or degenerate.
Tuning question for the harness — Open Q3.

Range compares **squared** distances, avoiding a `sqrt` in the hot loop (`sqrt` is on the
allowlist and safe, just unnecessary here). Towers deal instant damage on a
cooldown; no projectile travel in v1.

### Leaking and looping

A creep reaching the exit does two things on the same tick:

1. Removes one life from the **lane owner** (defender).
2. Returns to that same lane's spawn tiles and continues. **It does not despawn.**

**The sender gains nothing.** Lives only ever go down, for both players. This is the damping
term: without it a leak is a 2-point swing, the leader's life pool compounds alongside their
income, and there is likely no assignment of constants that keeps that non-degenerate.
*(Supersedes the earlier "sender credited on every lap" rule.)*

**The only thing that removes a creep is tower damage.** No lap cap, no decay, no timeout.

- **Damage persists across laps.** A creep never heals; its HP is a countdown across the
  whole match.
- **Each creep tracks `laps`, rendered on the model.** A creep on lap 5 is the most important
  object on the screen.

**The leak/kill race — measured at step 6, and both earlier claims were wrong.**

The first draft said "every creep dies eventually, so matches end on their own." The
correction said the opposite: "a creep whose HP exceeds your maze's damage-per-lap never
dies." Neither holds.

Because damage persists across laps and laps are unlimited, accumulated damage passes any
finite HP. **A creep taking any nonzero damage per lap always dies**, in roughly
`HP / damage-per-lap` laps. The only creep that truly never dies is one nothing ever shoots.

So the match is a **race**, and this is the number that decides it:

```
      laps-to-kill  =  creep HP / your maze's damage per lap
      lives-you-lose =  min(laps-to-kill, lives remaining)
```

Measured: one level-1 Single-target against a Tank II kills it — after taking **16 of the
defender's 20 lives**. Six towers on the same route cut that to a handful. The creep dies
either way; what the maze buys is how much it costs you.

That reframes the balance question usefully. It is not "can this creep be killed" but "does
killing it cost more lives than I have", which is a ratio the harness can measure directly
at step 8 rather than a qualitative judgement.

Two passive players who never send still never end a match — accepted for v1, fixed by the
v2 wave clock.

**Win condition.** Each team starts at **20 lives** (a working figure; the decision is "enough
that one bad leak is a crisis with time to respond"). First to zero loses, and the match ends
immediately — the sim stops accepting commands and both clients show the result. **If both
reach zero on the same tick, the match is a draw.** A match also ends on concede, or when a
socket closes: the remaining player wins.

### Economy

Two numbers per player: **gold in hand** and **income**.

- **Income pays into gold every 15 seconds** (300 ticks), starting at **25**.
- **Every creep you send permanently raises your income.** Cheaper creeps give more income per
  gold spent; stronger tiers give less. Sending is the only way income grows.
- **Killing a creep in your lane pays a small bounty** to you, the lane owner.
- **Selling a tower refunds a fraction of what was spent on it** and triggers a field rebuild.
- **Creep tiers unlock on a tick count** from match start, never a wall clock. There is no
  wall clock in the sim (`Date.now` is banned there), and wall-clock time would diverge across
  a lockstep stall.
- **Starting gold: 60** — enough for exactly one Single-target tower, so the opening move is a
  real choice rather than 40 seconds of waiting for the first income tick. A tuning constant.

### Towers (v1)

No elements, no research, no damage types. Three archetypes, each upgradable in place through
**three levels**; upgrading costs gold and raises damage and range.

| Tower | Damage | Range | Fire rate | Answers |
|-------|--------|-------|-----------|---------|
| **Single-target** | High per hit | Long | Slow | Tanks |
| **Splash** | Area, small radius | Short | Medium | Swarms |
| **Slow** | Low, applies a movement slow | Medium | Medium | Runners — multiplies the other two |

### Creeps (v1)

The mirror of the towers, differentiated only by HP, speed and count per purchase. **No armour
types, no abilities in v1.**

| Creep | Profile | Role |
|-------|---------|------|
| **Swarm** | Cheap, many per send, low HP | Overwhelms single-target |
| **Runner** | Fast, low HP | The looping threat |
| **Tank** | Slow, very high HP | Outlasts splash |

Stronger versions unlock on the timer.

### AI opponent

The bot does what a human does, not what a wave script does: places and upgrades a maze from
templates, splits each income tick between defence and sending by a target ratio, escalates
tiers as they unlock, and has an **emergency state** — when a creep is looping in its lane it
drops everything and stacks damage along that lane's current flow-field route.

**Difficulty is tuned through the spend ratio and reaction delay.**

**The bot emits `Input` records, exactly like a player.** `bot.ts` is a pure function of
`(state, seed)` returning the same `Build` / `Upgrade` / `Sell` / `Send` a human would send,
appended to the match log. It never mutates state directly. So a bot match replays on any
future version, and the bot is unit-testable by asserting on the Inputs it emits from a given
board.

**Known limitation, priced honestly:** template mazes do not respond to what is being sent at
them, and the two difficulty knobs make the bot flood *harder*, never maze *smarter*. The bot
is likely the mode that gets played most, so if it turns out flat, adaptive maze selection is
the first thing to build after v1.

**Measured after v1, and the prediction was backwards.** Adaptive *mazing* — answering the wave
in your lane with the tower that counters it — loses 0-12 to the fixed template across six
spend ratios in both seats. It is not neutral, it is worse than not looking: the fixed 3:1:1
mix answers all three creep shapes adequately, specialising answers the wave that is already
dying, and the bot only ever adds towers so every over-commitment is permanent. The half that
does work is the other one, counter-picking what to **send** at the opponent's maze, which wins
10-2 and is what shipped. Doing both wins only 8-4, because the bad half drags the good one
down. Pinned by `packages/harness/test/adaptive.test.ts`, which is written to fail if someone
later "fixes" this on the reasonable-sounding grounds that a bot reacting to threats must beat
one that does not.

### Tick and network

**Tick rate: 20Hz fixed** (50ms). The **driver** — the client's render loop, or the harness —
owns a `performance.now()` accumulator and calls `step()`; `packages/sim` never reads a clock,
which is why `Date.now` is banned inside it. Catch-up runs at most **10 ticks per frame**.

**Interpolation is clamped at 1.0.** The renderer draws creeps between the last two computed
ticks. Under strict wait the sim stops whenever a peer input is late, and an unclamped factor
would keep gliding creeps toward a tick that never happened — through towers, then snapping
back when the real tick lands. That reads as broken pathfinding rather than a slow network.
Clamp the factor, freeze motion, and let the `peer-stalled` overlay explain it.

**Delay window: negotiated, targeting 100-200ms.** Each client runs the sim locally for
instant feedback; the server relays every command to the other side and both apply it at the
same tick. At join each client measures RTT over 5 pings; the server sets
`delay = clamp(ceil(RTT_max / 50ms) + 2, 4, 20)` ticks — a 200ms floor, invisible for tower
placement, rising only when the connection demands it. Broadcast once before tick 0, never
changed during a match.

A *fixed* delay would be simpler but stalls whenever RTT exceeds it, and under strict-wait
lockstep that stall hits both players. Negotiation costs five pings at join and removes the
failure mode. **Bot matches run local with `delay = 1` and no server at all.**

**Lockstep is strict wait.** A client simulates tick `T` only when it holds both players'
inputs for `T`. An input therefore cannot arrive for an already-simulated tick — the failure
mode is *stalling*, not desync. If a peer is more than 40 ticks (2s) behind, both clients show
a `peer-stalled` overlay that clears itself when inputs resume.

Known weakness: backgrounded browser tabs throttle timers hard, so alt-tabbing stalls both
players. On the web this is a common path, not an edge case. Accepted for v1 with a visible
overlay; it is the strongest argument for an authoritative server at v2.

**Match start:** the server broadcasts `start` when both players are present; tick 0 begins
there. A room with one player sits in lobby and does not tick.

**Rematch is a new match, not a continuation.** Same room and same sockets, but tick resets to
0, a new seed is issued, the hash ring buffers clear, and `delay` is renegotiated — connection
quality may have changed, and a stale delay is exactly the value you do not want carried
forward silently.

**Emission rule.** At local tick `T` each client emits exactly one input — an action or
`Kind.None` — stamped for `T + delay`. Ticks `0 .. delay-1` are implicit `None` for both
players. Without this bootstrap, strict-wait lockstep deadlocks at tick 0.

**Input schema:**

```ts
enum Kind { None = 0, Build = 1, Upgrade = 2, Sell = 3, Send = 4 }

type Input =
  | { tick: number; player: 0 | 1; kind: Kind.None }
  | { tick: number; player: 0 | 1; kind: Kind.Build;   tower: number; x: number; y: number }
  | { tick: number; player: 0 | 1; kind: Kind.Upgrade; x: number; y: number }
  | { tick: number; player: 0 | 1; kind: Kind.Sell;    x: number; y: number }
  | { tick: number; player: 0 | 1; kind: Kind.Send;    creep: number }
```

`tower` and `creep` are indices into the data files, never names.

**Plain `enum`, never `const enum`.** A `const enum` has no runtime object and relies on
cross-file inlining that needs type information. esbuild does not read type information, and
both Vite and Vitest transpile through esbuild; `isolatedModules` (the TypeScript 6.0 default)
makes referencing an ambient const enum an error outright. A plain `enum` serializes to the
same numbers on the wire and works everywhere.

**`Kind.Sell`** refunds a fraction of spend and rebuilds the field. Free-form mazing means
constant misplacement; without sell, every mis-click permanently degrades the maze in the one
mechanic the game is about. The refund fraction is a tuning constant — too generous and
rebuilding the maze every wave becomes a strategy.

**Concede is a relay message, not a `Kind`.** It ends the match immediately, opponent wins,
rematch offered. It must live **outside** the command stream: a concede stamped for `T+delay`
cannot be applied while the sim is stalled waiting for a peer input, so under strict wait you
would be unable to concede exactly when your opponent has backgrounded their tab — the common
case the doc already names, and the precise situation concede exists for. The relay ends the
match on receipt and tells both clients; the sim never sees it.

`Kind.Concede` is therefore removed from the `Input` union. The realistic losing ending is a
player watching creeps lap for minutes and closing the tab, which already scores as the
opponent winning; concede is the same outcome, dignified and instant, and it is what makes
rematch reachable after a loss.

**Exactly one input per player per tick**, enforced server-side. **Total order within a tick:**
sorted by `player` ascending, then `kind` ascending.

**`Kind.None` is a watermark, not a per-tick message.** A client sends "no input through tick
T" every 10 ticks (twice a second), and the peer treats every tick up to T as `None`. An actual
command is sent immediately and carries its own watermark.

One message per tick per client would be ~2,400 messages per minute per match, forever, even
while both players idle — and with a 15-second decision cadence they idle most of the time.
That volume also defeats the **Hibernation API**, whose whole purpose is evicting memory
between messages: a message every 25ms means the Durable Object never hibernates, so "bills
near zero when idle" would be true of an empty room and false of a live match. The watermark
cuts wire traffic 10x and keeps the idle path genuinely idle.

`Kind.None` is stripped entirely when the log is persisted or turned into a match URL.

**All rule checks live inside `step()`** — gold, income, cooldowns, placement legality, sell
refunds, tier gating. Never in the client.

**Wire validation happens at the server, before relay.** `step()` guards *rules*; the server
guards *shape*: `tick` inside the delay window, `player` matching the socket, `x`/`y` inside
the grid, `tower`/`creep` indices in range for the loaded data version, one input per player
per tick. A failing frame is dropped, the sender told, the match continues.

**One validator, two callers.** `packages/sim` exports `validateShape(cmd, data)`; the server
imports it rather than re-deriving index bounds from the data files. The wire contract then
stays versioned with the data it validates against, and there is one place to change when a
creep type is added.

**Room codes:** 6 characters from an unambiguous alphabet (no `0/O`, no `1/l/I`) — about a
billion combinations, from `crypto.getRandomValues` (the relay, unlike the sim, may use real
randomness). Short enough to read aloud, long enough that a stranger does not stumble in.

**Collision handling is by rejection, not by lookup.** Durable Objects are addressed with
`idFromName(code)`: every name always resolves, there is no enumeration API, and asking whether
a code is taken would instantiate the object for it. So do not check — generate, and let the
object itself refuse a second lobby if one is already seated. At a billion codes and a handful
of concurrent matches, a collision is a curiosity, not a risk.

**Version handshake on connect.** Client sends protocol and data version; a mismatch is refused
with "reload, the game updated" rather than allowed to desync. This is the realistic failure —
a stale tab after a mid-session redeploy. Consequence: a deploy kills every live match and
there is no reconnect in v1. Accepted; deploy when nobody is playing.

Bot mode needs none of this — no wire, no untrusted input.

### Local prediction

Inputs apply `delay` ticks late (200ms-1s) and `step()` is the authority on legality. The
client closes that window by predicting and reconciling:

1. On click, call the **same exported legality predicate `step()` uses** — imported from
   `packages/sim/legality.ts`, never reimplemented.
2. If legal, draw the tower as a **translucent pending ghost**; the path preview updates as if
   it existed.
3. On confirmation the ghost becomes solid. On refusal it fades out **with the reason**.

**Client-side input queue.** The server accepts one command per player per tick, so two clicks
inside the same 50ms tick would lose the second. Drag-placing a run of towers is the core verb,
so the client queues locally and re-stamps each command to the next free tick rather than
dropping it. Ghosts render for every queued command, not only the one in flight.

**Three outcomes, not two.** A ghost carries the tick it was stamped for. Once the sim advances
past that tick with no matching command applied, the command is provably lost — the server
dropped it on validation, or the socket ate it — and the ghost fades with "command lost, try
again." Without this, a dropped frame leaves a translucent tower on screen forever and the game
looks like it is ignoring clicks, in the mechanic the player uses most.

No timers are needed: the client already knows `delay` and the current tick, so expiry is a
comparison, not a clock.

A pending tower must never look like a placed one. Prediction is client-only and never enters
the state hash.

### Determinism check

**Full canonical walk every tick.** FNV-1a over a canonical serialization: fields in declared
order, floats written through a `DataView` as explicit **little-endian Float64**, `-0`
normalized to `0`, NaN banned and asserted (a NaN in the sim is a bug).

~500 creeps × ~6 fields is ~3,000 integers; hashing that is microseconds against a 50ms budget,
so there is no performance case for an incremental digest — and an incremental digest would
create a far worse problem: an invariant requiring every mutation site to update a running
checksum, where one missed site is a silent hole in the only desync detector in the system.
*(Supersedes the earlier incremental-digest decision.)*

Computed every tick into a **40-entry ring buffer**, exchanged every 20 ticks. On mismatch,
walk the buffers back to the exact diverging tick, freeze both clients, show it. No resync.

**Cheating:** the relay is client-authoritative and trivially cheatable. Accepted for a game
played with friends; the server can run the identical `step()` later at no architectural cost.

### Desync dump

Freezing and naming the tick tells you *that* it broke, not *why* — the input log lives in
memory and dies with the tab.

**On desync both clients offer the match as a downloadable JSON file:** the full input log from
tick 0 (with `None` stripped), the seed, the negotiated `delay`, the data version, the
diverging tick, both peers' hash ring buffers around it, and each client's build version.

That file is exactly what `packages/harness/run.ts` consumes, so a desync at a friend's
house becomes
a headless local replay. The same dump is bound to a debug key during normal play, so a match
that merely *felt* wrong can be captured too.

This is the only observability in the project and it is enough: no backend, no telemetry, no
logging — what is needed is the ability to reproduce one specific failure.

### Tests

The golden fixture is the keystone, but end-to-end: when it goes red it says the match diverged,
not which system did it. It is paired with unit tests on the two categories most likely to
silently differ from what was agreed.

**Determinism-critical orderings:** flow-field neighbour iteration (N, E, S, W) and the
resulting field; targeting by lowest `dist` then creep id; stuck-creep snap ordering;
spatial-hash bucket iteration; creep update order.

**Rules settled by conversation, each needing an assertion that it survived implementation:**

- a leaked creep costs the defender one life, credits **nobody**, and respawns in the *same* lane
- damage persists across laps; a creep never heals
- **[REGRESSION]** a creep with no path teleports to its lane's spawn, keeping damage and lap
  count. Two superseded rules were tried first: charging three lives (beatable by any creep
  surviving more than three laps) and refusing the placement (which made legality depend on
  creep positions and handed the attacker a build-denial weapon). Assert the teleport, and
  assert that no placement is ever refused for a reason other than sealing spawn→exit
- a send releases one creep every 4 ticks, alternating spawn tiles — six Swarms must occupy
  six distinct positions, not one. Assert their positions differ after 24 ticks
- concede is handled by the relay and never appears in the command stream
- placement that would seal spawn→exit is refused; placement on an occupied tile is refused
- both teams at zero on the same tick is a draw
- income pays on schedule; sending raises income permanently; a kill pays the lane owner
- sell refunds the correct fraction and rebuilds the field
- concede ends the match immediately with the opponent as winner
- tier gating refuses a creep below its unlock time
- the bot emits only Inputs a player could legally submit

**The golden fixture must run on two genuinely different engines.** A committed command log
plus its expected final hash, asserted in CI under Node **and a non-V8 engine** —
Playwright/WebKit, Firefox, or Bun/JSC. Node plus headless Chrome are both V8 and would test
one engine while claiming two. This fixture, not the lint rule, is what actually guarantees
premise 3.

**The fixture pins its own frozen data set.** Tuning is hundreds of edits to `data/*.json`, and
every one changes the final hash. If the fixture reads live data you will regenerate it rather
than investigate it, which turns the keystone regression test into a rubber stamp. Commit
`test/golden/data-v1/` alongside the log and hash, and never edit it — a new fixture gets a new
frozen set.

**It starts at step 2, not step 9.** Ten lines and a CI matrix entry with one creep walking, so
determinism bugs surface as they are introduced rather than all at once after tuning.

**Remaining sim coverage** — every branch, all headless, no browser:

- `step()` dispatch across all six `Kind` values, including `None` as a no-op that still
  respects ordering
- flow field: candidate rebuild discarded on refusal (no partial mutation of the live field)
- targeting: range boundary exactly at the edge; cooldown timing
- economy: tier unlock at the exact boundary tick
- hash: `-0` normalizes to `0`; a NaN in state trips the assert
- `validateShape` rejects out-of-range `tower`/`creep` indices and out-of-grid `x`/`y`
- bot: spend ratio splits an income tick as configured; the emergency state triggers when a
  creep is looping and not before
- rematch resets tick to 0, clears the ring buffers, and reissues the seed

**Two client predicates, extracted as pure functions so they test headless.** Both only
misbehave during a network stall, so neither is findable by looking at the screen:

- `interpAlpha(elapsedMs, tickMs) => min(elapsed / tickMs, 1)` — never exceeds 1.0
- `ghostExpired(ghostTick, currentTick) => currentTick > ghostTick` — a command whose tick
  passed unconfirmed is lost

Keep the arithmetic in these functions and the renderer becomes a thin caller.

**Data invariants** — assert the balance files satisfy the rules the design states, not just
that they parse:

- cheaper creeps give **more** income per gold than expensive ones (the doc's core economic
  claim, and a one-character typo away from being false)
- each creep tier is strictly stronger than the tier below it
- upgrade costs increase with level; sell refund is a fraction below 1

You will edit these files hundreds of times during tuning. A bad number reads as bad balance
rather than as a bug, which is the most expensive kind of mistake to chase.

**Not covered by this tier:** the server (wire validation, handshake, hibernation), the
lockstep stall/resume path, three.js rendering, and the Zustand throttle. Integration- and
browser-shaped, deferred to TODOS.

**Framework:** Vitest. The repo has no test config yet — when `package.json` lands, add a
`## Testing` section to `CLAUDE.md` naming the command, so future reviews detect it.

## Recommended Approach

**Approach A**, resequenced so the deployed URL exists on day one (premise 2) and the bot —
"the first genuinely fun build" — arrives before any netcode.

**pnpm monorepo.** The server imports the same sim the browser runs, which is what makes a
later authoritative server a config change rather than a rewrite.

```
packages/sim/          pure TS, zero three.js, zero DOM
  state.ts             Team entity, full game state
  step.ts              (state, commands[]) -> state, at 20Hz
  field.ts             flow field: Dijkstra from exit, dist/dir, no-block predicate
  towers.ts            spatial hash, targeting by lowest dist
  creeps.ts            movement, laps, leaks, traps
  economy.ts           gold, income, bounty, sell, tier unlocks
  bot.ts               AI opponent — emits commands, never mutates state
  legality.ts          shared predicate, imported by the client for prediction
  hash.ts              canonical LE serialization + FNV-1a + 40-tick ring buffer
  rng.ts               mulberry32 — bot tie-breaks only; delete if unused
  data/                towers.json · creeps.json · economy.json   (versioned)
  test/                unit (orderings + settled rules) · golden/match-01.log + .hash

packages/client/       Vite + TypeScript
  render/              three.js — InstancedMesh for creeps and projectiles;
                       reads sim state per frame, interpolates between ticks
  ui/                  React overlay: menus, lobby, build/send panels
  store.ts             Zustand — a small snapshot (gold, income, lives, laps)
                       pushed a few times a second. Sim state never enters React.
  net.ts · input.ts

packages/server/       Cloudflare Workers + Durable Objects (NOT Node — no Node stdlib)
  index.ts             Worker entry, routes /r/:code to the match Durable Object
  match.ts             the DO: lobby, start, RTT negotiation, WebSocketPair,
                       Hibernation API, wire validation, ordered relay

packages/harness/      Node + TypeScript
  run.ts               headless bot-vs-bot match runner (imports packages/sim directly).
                       Node, not Workers — it never opens a socket, and it needs a filesystem
```

**Sim state stays out of React.** The renderer reads sim state every frame; React only ever
sees a throttled snapshot in the Zustand store. Putting per-tick state into React state is
the standard way to make a game UI stutter.

Testing: **Vitest**, running the sim headlessly with no browser at all.

### Interface

Reference layout: `docs/designs/duel-screen-wireframe.png`, source in
`duel-screen-wireframe.html` beside it so it stays regenerable.

**Screen budget.** Two 40 × 24 lanes side by side at 26px would be 2080px wide and does not fit
a laptop. So: **your lane renders at full size; the opponent's renders as a live scaled view**
at roughly 40%. Whether a scaled view carries the counter-picking read — premise-level, "you
see their maze and send what exploits it" — is genuinely open and is the first thing to check
once it renders (Open Q5).

Load-bearing decisions, recorded so they survive the picture:

- **Upgrading lives in an inline panel on the field.** Click a placed tower: current vs next
  damage/range/rate, cost, upgrade button, sell button, range ring while selected. Three levels
  per tower makes upgrading the most frequent mid-match verb after placing, and the panel keeps
  your eyes on the maze. Tower level is **also** drawn on the tower itself, so maze strength is
  readable without clicking.
- **Lap count renders as instanced pips, not numerals.** Small markers (dots, or a ring
  segment) beside the creep, drawn from the same `InstancedMesh` as the creep bodies, with
  `instanceColor` carrying the under-pressure red. Text is not instanceable: five hundred
  numeral labels is five hundred draw calls, and the count peaks exactly when the field is
  most crowded and the budget tightest — a losing position, where nearly every creep is on a
  high lap. Pips cost zero extra draw calls and read faster than a numeral mid-fight.
  Precision fades past four or five laps; you know the creep is bad without knowing it is on
  lap 9, which is the right trade at a glance. An SDF text atlas is the upgrade if numerals
  ever earn their keep.
- **Leak trail:** the route the looping creep keeps taking, drawn as a fading line on exit.
- **Maze score** (`Maze: 214 tiles`) in the lane label, for both players.
- **Send palette has three states:** affordable, unaffordable (`need 28g`), not-yet-unlocked
  (`unlocks 1:22`).
- **Two clocks in the HUD:** `Next pay` (15s income) and `Next tier`. No wave timer — this game
  has no waves, only sends.
- **Build and Send stay separate palettes.** Opposite-facing economies.
- **A pending predicted tower never looks like a placed one.**
- **The room URL is permanently in the HUD.**

**Path preview cost.** One candidate field rebuild per hovered *tile* — rebuild on tile-change
only, never pixel-change, and cache. If insufficient, debounce to 100ms.

### Unresolved decision

Four capabilities fall out of the deterministic command log almost for free — **replay
recording to object storage, replay playback with a scrubber, spectator links, and the full
AI-vs-AI sweep harness**. Whether any of them belong in v1 was put to the builder and not
answered.

**Standing position: all four remain deferred to v2**, which is where this review previously
placed them. This is not a decision, it is the absence of one — nothing was defaulted
silently. The cheapest of the four is recording (the server already relays every command, so
recording is a write), and it is the one that unlocks the other three later without touching
the protocol.

**~6-8 weeks of evenings, or 4-6 days of focused CC time.**

The earlier "2-3 days" figure was too low, and the sequencing decisions were made against it.

## Open Questions

1. **Replay versioning.** A log recorded against `creeps.json` v1 will not replay after a
   balance change. v1 stamps the data version into every log; pinned historical data sets are a
   v2 problem.
2. **Creep population ceiling.** Bounded only by gold; nothing but damage removes a creep.
   Render cost grows. Target 500 creeps at 20Hz inside 25ms, unmeasured.
3. **Economy and tuning constants.** Income curve, income-per-gold by tier, bounty, sell refund
   fraction, spawn-queue interval (4 ticks is a guess), tier unlock ticks, starting lives (20)
   and starting gold (60), maze length targets, tower costs. Also: does always-target-lowest-`dist`
   plus long-lived tanks produce a degenerate aggro-soak? All unchosen, all for the harness.
3b. **Aggro soak — now measured, not hypothetical.** The step 4 golden fixture shows a
   creep completing **two full laps past ten towers at full health**, never targeted once,
   because targeting picks the creep nearest the exit and in a tight pack that is always the
   leader. So the concern is real rather than speculative. Open: whether it is a feature
   (the leader tanks, and packing creeps is a genuine tactic) or degenerate (a free rider in
   every group). Candidate fixes if it reads badly: target the lowest-HP creep in range
   instead, spread targeting across a tower's cooldown, or let splash punish packing harder.
4. **Is a non-decided midgame reachable at all?** Creep HP is uncapped and escalates on a timer;
   tower power caps at three levels. Lives only decrease, nothing but damage removes a creep,
   and income compounds. So the first creep your maze cannot kill may decide the match minutes
   before it ends, leaving a long known-lost tail that concede papers over rather than fixes.
   This is not "pick better constants" — it is a question about whether *any* constants work
   against an uncapped-HP-versus-capped-DPS race. **The harness needs a "match decided" metric**
   (the tick after which the eventual loser never regains a life) as its first measurement, and
   if the answer is no, the fix is structural: a fourth tower tier, tower damage scaling with
   creep tier, or a cap on creep HP growth.
5. **Reconnect.** v1 ends the match on socket close, and a deploy kills live matches. The main
   reason to adopt Colyseus at v2.
6. **Does a scaled opponent view carry counter-picking?** Premise-level read on a 40%-scale
   lane. Check as soon as it renders.
7. **Background-tab throttling.** Alt-tabbing stalls both players under lockstep. Visible overlay
   in v1; the strongest argument for an authoritative server at v2 — which the monorepo and the
   shared sim package make cheap when the time comes.

## Success Criteria

**v1 is done when:** you open a deployed URL and either play the bot immediately or send a room
code to a friend; you both build serpentine mazes from three tower types, upgrading and selling

**Out of scope for v1:** art, sound, armour types, creep abilities, elements, research, tech

## Distribution Plan

- **Static client** on Cloudflare Pages / Vercel / Netlify. Free tier is fine — static hosting
  does not cold-start, and bot matches need no server at all.
- **Relay: Cloudflare Durable Objects — one object per match.** It holds the WebSockets, bills
  near zero when idle, and has no spin-down. That last property matters more than it sounds:
  a Fly/Railway/Render free tier cold-starts in tens of seconds, which breaks premise 2
  outright (your friend clicks, sees nothing, closes the tab) and was the reason this doc
  previously listed an always-on paid box as launch-blocking. Durable Objects removes both the
  cold start and the ~$5/mo. **The launch-blocking hosting risk is resolved.**
  - Fallback if you would rather not be on Cloudflare: one small box on Fly.io or Lightsail
    running a Docker container. A single box handles hundreds of concurrent 1v1 matches.
  - A game relay is a long-lived stateful connection and is a poor fit for Lambda. Do not.
- **The server is a relay in v1.** Because it imports the same TypeScript sim, it can later run
  the match and become authoritative with no rewrite — the single strongest argument for the
  monorepo.
- Deploy on merge to `main` via GitHub Actions. CI runs Vitest and the cross-engine golden
  fixture.
- **Distribution: web first**, PWA for install-from-browser, Capacitor if and when you want App
  Store presence.
- **Persistence: none in v1.** Guest IDs only, no accounts, no ratings. When it is time:
  replays are tiny (a seed plus a command log) so they go to object storage as JSON (R2 or S3);
  match metadata, ratings and accounts go to Postgres (Neon or Supabase); SQLite on the box is
  fine until there are actually users. See the unresolved decision below on whether recording
  starts in v1.
- The room URL is the entire distribution channel.

8. **Tune.** Use the harness. Expect this to take a while; it is the longest pole.
   **Done in part.** The stalemate is fixed: every spend ratio from 0.1 to 0.8 now resolves,
   where below 0.2 they used to run forever. The fix was structural rather than numerical —
   the creep roster became a growth rule instead of a six-entry list, and HP per gold now
   *rises* as you buy up, because towers are permanent and creeps die once (measured: a 7,900g
   maze deals 128,861 damage a lap, ~16x the HP that gold buys in creeps). Two defects turned up
   on the way: the golden fixture was not pinning its balance data despite six steps of claiming
   to, and the bounty invariant checked per creep but not per wave, so a swarm could refund 60%
   of its cost to the defender. What is left is pacing — mirror matches run ~30 minutes and the
   whole contest lands in the last minute — and a difficulty ladder that has to be re-derived by
   search after each balance change. Both are written up in `TODOS.md` with the measurements.
   **Step 7 already found this step's headline item and it is worse than "numbers need
   tweaking": below a 0.2 spend ratio, matches do not end at all.** Both bots reach 20,000
   income and 1,800 sends across 33 minutes of game time and stay on 11 lives, because tower
   upgrades outscale creeps as income compounds. Above 0.25 the same matchup finishes in 43
   seconds. There is no middle. Full measurements in `TODOS.md` under "Defence outscales
   offence"; the fix lives in `creeps.json`, `towers.json` and the income rule, and a match
   timeout is explicitly not the fix.
9. **Determinism, the rest of it: `hash.ts`, ring buffer, desync dump.** The golden fixture
   already exists from step 2 and has been growing since; this adds the per-tick hash, the
   40-tick ring buffer, the peer exchange and the downloadable dump.
   **Done, except the wire.** `desync.ts` has the 40-entry ring and `findDivergence`, which
   returns the EARLIEST disagreeing tick — every tick after a divergence is also wrong, so the
   newest mismatch is the least informative one. `dump.ts` writes a match to JSON and
   `harness replay` runs it back, reporting which peer this machine agrees with; that verdict
   is the whole point and is covered by a test that perturbs one run and confirms the replay
   sides with the clean one. The dump carries the balance data itself, not just its version,
   for the same reason the golden fixture now does.
   One correctness fix fell out: `Command.tick` was never authoritative — `step()` ignores it
   and the two producers disagreed, the local queue stamping `tick + 1` and the bot stamping
   `tick` — so the driver rewrites it to the tick a command is actually applied on. A replay
   trusting the raw field would have placed half the log a tick late.
   What is not exercised yet is the peer exchange, because there is no peer until step 10.
   `Driver.checkPeer()` is the seam: feed it the peer's ring and it freezes on a mismatch and
   reports `no-overlap` as the stall it is.
10. **Server and 1v1.** **Built, not deployed** — the relay needs a Cloudflare account, so
    `pnpm --filter @ltw/server deploy` is the one step left. Everything else is in and tested:
    lobby, `start`, RTT negotiation, wire validation, version handshake, ordered relay with
    watermarks, room codes, join-by-URL, socket close, out-of-band concede, rematch.
    **Local prediction is the piece NOT built** — see `TODOS.md`.
    Two corrections to this document fell out of building it. First, the watermark cadence
    cannot be a fixed 10 ticks: a client can only honestly promise "nothing through `T + delay`",
    so one watermark buys exactly `delay` ticks of progress, and with the delay flooring at 4
    a fixed cadence of 10 deadlocks both clients permanently — at exactly the connection quality
    two people on one network produce. It is `min(10, delay)` now. Second, a command must not be
    treated as its own promise: the relay can drop a frame, and the peer then waits forever on a
    tick the sender believes it promised, so the watermark goes out on its own cadence
    regardless.
    Original wording follows: Durable Object per match: lobby, `start`, RTT negotiation, wire
    validation, version handshake, ordered relay with `None` watermarks, room codes,
    join-by-URL, socket close, out-of-band concede, rematch, local prediction with an input
    queue.
11. **Play it with a friend.** **Ready, pending one command you have to run.** Local
    prediction is built — the ghost tower, the client-side re-stamping of a burst of clicks
    onto free ticks, and all three outcomes including "command lost", which is the one worth
    not skipping. The lobby, join-by-URL, concede and rematch are wired and were exercised
    against a real Worker.
    Three bugs came out of actually opening it, and none of them could have been found any
    other way. **The client never adopted its seat**: the scene is built before anyone knows
    which seat they get, so player 1 sent every command stamped `player: 0` and the relay
    refused all of them — on screen, a ghost tower that appeared and faded with a refusal,
    every time. **The Durable Object forgot the room whenever it hibernated**, because the
    seating lived in a field and hibernation is the whole reason the hosting is free; connect
    one player, wait, connect the second, and the second was seated as player 0 in a fresh room
    while the first waited for an opponent already present. It reproduced only with a
    human-length pause between joins, which is every real match and no fast test. And
    **"Create a room" also started a bot match**, because a selector of `#start button` had
    quietly grown to include the new lobby buttons.
    What is left is `wrangler login` and `pnpm --filter @ltw/server deploy`.

Steps 1-7 need no server at all. The relay only appears at step 10, by which point you already
know whether the game is worth networking.
